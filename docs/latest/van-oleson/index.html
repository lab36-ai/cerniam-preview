One question / Thirty years

I have spent three decades working on one question:

How can enterprise systems understand what is happening now, determine what matters, protect what must continue, and coordinate the right action in real time?

The answer has never belonged to one technology, one company, or one person. It has emerged through teams building operational systems where delay, failure, and lost context carry real consequences.

02 / From bespoke to broadly accessible

Built for the largest operations.
Now within reach of the mid-market.

For thirty years, operational nervous systems of this class were largely bespoke capabilities for airlines, logistics networks, global retailers, financial markets, and other large, well-funded enterprises.

The economics and technology have changed. Cloud infrastructure, real-time data platforms, knowledge graphs, and modern AI now make it possible to democratize these capabilities for mid-market companies.

Mid-market leaders face the same essential challenges: disconnected systems, decisions delayed by reconciliation, value leaking unnoticed, customer signals hidden across departments, capacity left unused, and opportunities missed. They should not need the budget or engineering organization of the world’s largest corporations to understand and act on their own operations.

04 / The foundational idea / Since 1997

The event was never the point.

An event is evidence that state changed. Its value comes from understanding that change inside the broader world around it.

In airline operations, an aircraft event does not stand alone. Its meaning depends on the flight, airport, crew, passengers, baggage, maintenance, weather, gates, downstream connections, service commitments, and every other state that may be affected.

Since 1997, the research focus has been to react to state change, place it inside a living operational context, derive actionable value from that world model, and coordinate the right response. Modern Operational AI extends that same idea. It does not replace the event system. It gives the changing world around the event more memory, meaning, and reasoning power.

05 / A pioneering team inside the enterprise

The credit was for event innovation. The deeper invention was context.

At Delta Air Lines, our enterprise engineering team and Georgia Tech systems researchers were credited with innovating event methods from inside a large-scale enterprise. But the event was never the main point. The true innovation was the real-time operational model around it.

An event told us something changed. The operational model told us what it meant, what else was affected, and what the enterprise could do next.

WIESS 2000 / USENIXOperational Information Systems: An Example from the Airline Industry

Presented Delta’s mid-tier OIS approach: minimally intrusive taps captured transactions from legacy operational systems for consistent replay, processing, and event-based dissemination across a geographically distributed enterprise. The work joined electronic-commerce enablement with the hard requirements of real-time operational applications.

Developed middleware-level mirroring that could use application semantics to filter, coalesce, and vary streaming-data consistency as client demand changed. The goal was scalable service without sacrificing current state or quality of service.

06 / Return to applied research

From leading enterprise platforms back to the research question.

After leading technology at L Brands and Victoria’s Secret, and after deploying AI applications across retail operations and customer experience, Oleson stepped away from the CTO role to return to applied research.

The objective was not to place an assistant above disconnected enterprise software. It was to design the operating architecture beneath intelligent action: live events, semantic context, machine reasoning, explicit policy, human authority, and verifiable outcomes working as one system.
